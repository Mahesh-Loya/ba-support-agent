# Report — AI Support Agent for British Airways

All numbers below are pulled directly from `reports/results.json`, generated by
`python -m src.cli evaluate` against the 198-example hand-labelled golden set
(`data/golden/golden.jsonl`) and the 100-example quality set
(`data/golden/quality.jsonl`). Nothing here is estimated or rounded up from a
smaller run — reproduce it yourself with `python -m src.cli reproduce`.

## 1. Problem framing

**What "good" means for British Airways.** BA was chosen over more obvious
candidates (AppleSupport, AmazonHelp) by measurement, not instinct: across the
full 2.81M-row dataset, BA has the highest support-density (39.1% of customer
messages contain a concrete problem) and the lowest deflection-to-DM rate
(14.0%) of any major brand, versus AppleSupport's 52.5% deflection and 75.4%
link-dumping. A brand whose real replies mostly say "please DM us" or drop a
bare URL gives an agent nothing worth grounding a reply in. BA's replies are
short, specific, and usually resolve something — so "good" here means: match
that register, ground every claim in what BA actually did for a similar case,
and never invent a commitment (a refund, an upgrade, a rebooking) that the
historical record doesn't support.

**What we chose not to build.** Multi-turn conversation state, tool-calling
against a live booking system, fine-tuning, a web UI, retrieval reranking,
and multilingual support. Each would have improved some metric, but the
brief's stated grading axis is proof of trustworthiness, not agent
sophistication — time went into the evaluation harness instead. See §6 for
what we'd build next.

## 2. Results vs. two baselines

| System | Accuracy | 95% CI | Macro-F1 | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Our agent** (LLM classify) | **63.6%** | [56.6%, 70.2%] | **57.0%** | [49.3%, 63.7%] |
| Simple (TF-IDF + logistic regression, no LLM) | 43.9% | [36.9%, 51.0%] | 34.7% | [27.5%, 41.4%] |
| Trivial (always guess the majority intent) | 24.7% | [18.7%, 30.8%] | 4.4% | [3.5%, 5.2%] |

n = 198 for every row. The agent's confidence interval does not overlap the
simple baseline's (56.6% > 51.0%), so this is a real, not a noise-level,
difference. The agent clearly earns its keep over both baselines on intent
classification.

**Escalation decision vs. the human label:** 77.8% accuracy [72.2%, 83.8%],
macro-F1 66.8% [58.5%, 74.5%] — the agent's auto-vs-escalate call agrees with
what the human labeller said about 4 times out of 5.

**Reply quality**, per the LLM judge, across all 198 drafted replies:

| Axis | Rate |
|---|---|
| grounded | 66.7% |
| helpful | 59.6% |
| on_brand | 80.8% |
| no_overpromise | 95.5% |
| **harm rate** (ungrounded OR overpromising) | **33.8%** |

Read that last number carefully before treating any of this as a finished
product — see §4.

## 3. The headline deployment number

At cost ratio k=20 (a bad auto-reply costs 20x an unnecessary escalation),
the chosen threshold is 0.76:

> **The agent can safely auto-handle 8.6% of volume, at a 23.5% harm rate
> among what it auto-handles.**

The threshold barely moves across k=5 through k=20 (all land at 0.76,
coverage 8.6%) — once harm is weighted at all seriously, the system becomes
very conservative. Only at k=2 (harm treated as barely worse than an
unnecessary escalation) does coverage jump to 80.8%, at a 35.6% harm rate.
There is no threshold in the sensitivity sweep that gives both high coverage
and a low harm rate — that gap is the honest finding of this project, not a
tuning failure.

**Calibration** explains part of why the threshold has to be so
conservative. The raw LLM confidence score is badly miscalibrated
(ECE = 0.269): it reports confidence in the 0.85–0.92 range while actual
accuracy in that range is only 0.50–0.68 (see
`reports/figures/reliability.png`) — the model is overconfident. Blending
in retrieval similarity and the baseline classifier's margin into a single
routing score fixes most of this (ECE = 0.058, a 4.6x improvement) — a
design choice validated by data, not assumption.

## 4. "What is misleading about my headline number?"

- **63.6% intent accuracy does not mean 63.6% of volume can be automated.**
  It's a classification score, not a deployment number. The actual
  deployment number is 8.6% at a defensible harm rate — 7x lower than the
  headline accuracy might suggest to a skimming reader.
- **The judge itself should not be trusted as ground truth.** §5's judge-
  agreement analysis found near-zero (and in two cases negative) Cohen's
  kappa between the LLM judge and the human labeller. That means the 66.7%
  "grounded" and 59.6% "helpful" numbers above are generated by an
  instrument we've just shown doesn't reliably track human judgment on this
  task. Report them, but weight them accordingly.
- **n=198 is not a lot of statistical power.** The risk-coverage curve
  (`reports/figures/risk_coverage.png`) is visibly jagged at low coverage,
  because the harm-rate estimate at, say, 3% coverage is computed over only
  5-6 examples. Any specific point on that curve below ~30% coverage should
  be read as "roughly this," not "exactly this."
- **The simple baseline's training labels come from the LLM classifier
  itself**, not from independent human labels (no human-labelled corpus
  exists at the 2,000-example scale this baseline needs). That means the
  "simple" baseline in §2 inherits some of the LLM classifier's own biases,
  making the agent-vs-baseline gap look *smaller* than a truly independent
  baseline would show — if anything, this understates the agent's real
  advantage, but it means the comparison is not as clean as it looks.
- **BA's 2017 Twitter register may not transfer to Hiver's actual inbox
  product.** Twitter replies are short, public, and time-pressured in a way
  email/helpdesk replies aren't. A number that looks good here is not
  automatically a number that holds in a different channel.
- **Grounding on historical BA replies inherits BA's own deflections.**
  14% of BA's historical replies just say "DM us." "Matches what BA
  historically did" is not the same claim as "resolved the customer's
  problem" — our drafter can match BA's *style* of deflecting without that
  being a good outcome.
- **The corpus is from October–December 2017.** BA's policies, staffing,
  and tone have surely changed since; performance on 2017 data does not
  certify performance on 2026 traffic.

## 5. Judge-human agreement — the mandatory validation, and it's not good news

100 of BA's actual historical replies were independently judged by the
human labeller (on 4 axes) and by the LLM judge, on the exact same text.

| Axis | Cohen's κ | Raw agreement | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| grounded | **-0.020** | 52% | poor (worse than chance) |
| helpful | 0.071 | 52% | slight |
| on_brand | 0.030 | 61% | slight |
| no_overpromise | **-0.019** | 89% | poor (worse than chance) |

**This is a genuinely weak result, and it is reported as one.** Two things
are happening here, and they are different problems:

1. **On `no_overpromise`**, raw agreement is high (89%) but kappa is
   negative. This is the classic high-prevalence kappa paradox: the human
   labelled 99% of replies "no_overpromise = true" (BA staff are trained
   not to promise specific things on Twitter, so this is plausibly a real,
   near-universal property of the corpus, not a labelling error — see the
   golden set's own decision log). When one rater's answer is almost always
   the same value, kappa's chance-correction term inflates and the
   statistic becomes numerically unstable regardless of how good the judge
   actually is. High raw agreement, near-zero kappa, on a near-constant
   human rater: expected behaviour of the metric, not proof the judge is
   useless on this axis specifically.
2. **On `grounded`, `helpful`, and `on_brand`**, raw agreement is only
   52-61% — well below the 89%+ we'd see if the judge simply echoed the
   human's (skewed) label. The judge is *actively disagreeing* with the
   human on these more subjective axes, roughly as often as it agrees. This
   is a real, substantive finding: the LLM-as-judge, as built here, is not
   a reliable stand-in for human quality judgment on the axes that matter
   most for trusting a reply.

**Practical consequence:** every "reply quality" number in §2 and §3 that
came from the judge (grounded, helpful, harm rate) should be treated as a
weak, noisy proxy, not ground truth. The one number in this whole report
that does NOT depend on the judge — intent accuracy vs. the two baselines
(§2) — is the most trustworthy headline in this document for exactly that
reason.

## 6. Failure analysis — top 5 modes, with real examples

**1. The agent auto-handles messages the human said needed a person — 38 of
198 (19.2%).** The single largest concrete risk in this system. Examples,
verbatim from `reports/predictions.jsonl`:

> *"currently sitting Milan Linate airport with no bag as it missed my
> flight. Can you help me out..."* → agent auto-handled with a generic DM
> request, reason logged as `high_confidence_routine`.

**Hypothesis:** the hard-rule regex for lost baggage expects phrasing like
"lost bag" / "missing baggage." "no bag as it missed my flight" describes
the same real situation in different words and slips through the net. This
is a coverage gap in the safety-rule regex, not a confidence-threshold
problem — the fix is broader rule phrasing, not a stricter threshold.

**2. Repeated/persistent complaints get auto-handled as routine.**

> *"I will never again fly another airline... This is the second time..."*
> → auto-handled with a generic "sorry to hear, can you share more
> details?"

**Hypothesis:** the classifier's confidence measures "how sure am I this is
`complaint_feedback`," not "how upset or repeat-offended is this customer."
Confidence and severity are different axes, and only the first is measured.

**3. Complaints about things BA cannot control still get treated as
actionable.**

> *"another 5am arrival, another day half the egates are close... Poor
> [Border Force]"* → agent replies as if BA owns the e-gates, which are run
> by the airport/Border Force, not the airline.

**Hypothesis:** the retriever finds textually similar past complaints and
the drafter mimics BA's tone without a check for "is this actually BA's
domain to respond to."

**4. `other` vs. `praise_or_social` is the single most confused intent
pair (11 of 198).** Casual banter, marketing reactions, and ambiguous
one-liners get split unpredictably between the two.

**Hypothesis:** flagged as a real taxonomy limitation during design — the
boundary between "not a support request" and "social chit-chat that's still
not a support request" was never going to be crisp from clustering alone,
and it shows up directly in the confusion matrix.

**5. `complaint_feedback` confused with `booking_management` /
`flight_disruption` (8 + 6 = 14 of 198).** A message that both vents about a
bad experience *and* references a concrete problem (a broken booking flow, a
disrupted flight) gets pulled toward whichever category's keywords are more
salient in the text, rather than being reliably picked by intent.

**Hypothesis:** consistent with the taxonomy's own documented limitation
(§ decision log) that `flight_disruption` and `refund_compensation` don't
separate cleanly either — venting-plus-problem messages are inherently
multi-label, and a single-label taxonomy will always lose information on
them.

## 7. What we'd do with one more week

1. **Fix the lost-baggage regex gap from §6.1 first** — it's the one
   finding with a direct, cheap, high-value fix and a real safety cost if
   left alone.
2. **Re-run the judge-agreement study with a genuinely balanced quality
   sample** (deliberately oversampling replies likely to be flawed, rather
   than a stratified-but-still-skewed random sample) so kappa isn't fighting
   a near-constant human rater on 3 of 4 axes.
3. **Add a severity signal independent of classification confidence** —
   §6.2 and §6.3 are both cases where the model was confident about the
   *category* but blind to *how much this needs a human*. A small
   keyword/sentiment severity score, separate from intent confidence, is a
   cheap addition that directly targets the largest failure mode (§6.1's
   19.2% dangerous-auto-handle rate).
4. **Multi-label intents**, or at least a secondary "also touches on"
   field, to address §6.4/§6.5 rather than forcing every message into one
   bucket.
5. **A genuinely independent baseline** — hand-label a few hundred more
   corpus examples (or use Banking77 as a sanity-check dataset) so the
   simple baseline in §2 isn't trained on the same LLM's own labels.
